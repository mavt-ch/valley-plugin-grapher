# Grapher

Plot functions in Cartesian, polar, parametric, 3D, cylindrical, spherical and complex coordinates, with live sliders and a zoomable graph.

## Use

Open **Grapher** in the left sidebar. The dropdown in the header picks the view; each one keeps its own expressions. On flat views the **ℝ | π | ° | 10ⁿ** switch beside it labels the axes in real numbers, in multiples of π (π/2, π, 2π …), in degrees (90°, 180° …; the values stay radians) or in scientific notation (5·10⁻¹, 10³); only the labels change, so trigonometric functions use the Cartesian view with π selected (an older Trigonometric sheet opens there). On the Cartesian and parametric views a **scale** button (lin, log y, log x, log) beside it picks linear axes, a logarithmic y- or x-axis (semi-log) or both (log–log): a logarithmic axis has a line at every decade labelled 10ⁿ, its 2–9 multiples between them, no zero line, and values at or below zero are off the scale (the kernel samples evenly on the screen, so every decade is drawn as finely as the next; an integral's area closes along the bottom of the view on a logarithmic y-axis). The label switch keeps labelling the axes that stay linear; the scale is saved per view as `scales` in `graphs.json`, and a view's saved camera for the other scale starts afresh:

Each Grapher tab keeps its own graph: every view's sheet, the chosen view, the axis labels and the last 2D view or 3D camera of each sheet (`cameras` in `graphs.json`, saved when a movement stops). A tab opened fresh (+ in the tab strip, ⌘-click on a view or the open button) gets a new graph on that view with its example rows; a tab opened without an instance id (the rail, an older layout) is re-keyed to a copy of the main graph. Tabs are titled Grapher and show the icon of their view. At most 200 graphs are kept; past that, the graphs of tabs that are no longer open make room, oldest first. Properties has a **Notes** tab beside Reference (its icon is Material Icons "edit_note" by Google, Apache-2.0): one Markdown note per graph in a plain full-height text area, kept as written in `notes`, saved a moment after typing stops and on leaving the field; the eye button in its header shows the note through the host's Markdown reading view. Named profiles were removed (19 Nov): an older file keeps every graph, and a tab that was pointed at another graph (`tabs`) keeps showing it, while `names`, `icons` and `order` are dropped on the next save; the realm's `.valley/assets/icons/grapher-icon/` folder is no longer read and is left as it is.

Each expression row ends in an arrow that opens its menu (right-click opens the same one): **Bookmark**, **Change color** (a flyout of the palette colours in the chosen order) and Delete; the swatch at the start of the row hides and shows the graph. Bookmark files a copy of the expression, with the definitions it reads (its sliders such as `m = 1` and functions such as `f(x) = …`, found through the kernel's analysis), in the bookmark list: at the top level, in a group or in a subgroup (each ticked where the expression already is; picking a ticked one takes it out again), or in a **New group…** that opens the list to name it. The **bookmark** button beside ℝ | π (Font Awesome's bookmark, outlined while closed and filled while open) shows the list in place of the expressions, drawn like Map's Favorites: a bar with expand/collapse all and a new-group button, then groups with one level of subgroups (chevron, a folder icon that is closed or open like Map's, name, count and a ⋯ menu with Add subgroup, Rename, Notes and Delete group) and the bookmarks in them (colour, typeset expression, the names of the sliders saved with it, the icon of the view it was written for and a ⋯ menu with Show or Hide, Copy to expressions, **Edit expression** (an inline field: Enter saves, Escape cancels, and new definitions it reads are taken from the focused graph's sheet), **Change color**, Move to and Delete bookmark; both edits are undoable and never change the expressions). Rows cannot be text-selected, and a mouse click leaves no focus ring. Rows drag onto another row's upper or lower half, into a group, or below the list for the top level; a group never becomes a third level. While the list is open the graph tab shows the graph's **bookmark view** instead of its expressions, never a mix of the two: its own view (picked with the header's view button while the list is open), its own pan, zoom and orbit, and only the bookmarks picked there, blank while none is. A click on a bookmark shows it there with the sliders and functions saved with it, switching the bookmark view to the bookmark's view; a second click hides it. A shown bookmark is marked with a filled swatch and a tick, never a highlighted row, and only on the view shown. The expressions and their view stay exactly as they were and come back when the list closes; the bookmark view keeps its pick for the next time (saved per graph as `bookmarkViews` with `mode` and `shown` bookmark ids). The ⋯ menu has Show or Hide, **Copy to expressions** (the one way a bookmark reaches the expressions: it is added to the end of the expressions of its view with the saved definitions they lack, a slider they already have keeps its value), Move to and Delete bookmark. A folder whose bookmarks (subfolders included) all belong to one view shows that view's icon: a click on it shows every bookmark in it at once in the bookmark view (switching it to that view), ticks the folder, and the next click hides them again; its chevron only opens and closes it. A folder that mixes views only opens and closes on a click, and its bookmarks are shown one at a time. Clicking a folder (or its chevron, or Notes in its menu, which also opens Properties) points Properties → Notes at the folder's own notes: the Notes header then has a Graph | Folder switch, a folder with notes shows a note icon beside its count, and closing the list goes back to the graph's notes. Escape or the header's bookmark goes back to the expressions. Bookmarks are copies shared by every graph: deleting the expression, or closing its tab, leaves them; deleting a group moves its bookmarks and subgroups up a level (⌘Z restores it). They are saved as `bookmarks` (`groups` with `id`, `name`, an optional `parent`, `collapsed` and `note`, and `items` with `id`, `mode`, the `row`, optional `needs` (the saved definitions, at most 50) and an optional `group`) in `graphs.json`, at most 500 bookmarks and 200 groups. A file without `bookmarks` (a new realm, or one from before bookmarks) starts with example bookmarks, all self-contained: Functions (with Calculus and Fields and level curves), Curves, 3D (with Integrals and Cylindrical and spherical) and Complex numbers, named in the user's language (`src/bookmarkExamples.ts`); once the list exists, deleting every bookmark leaves it empty. The expression folders of an earlier version are gone: an older file keeps every expression of a folder in its place, hidden when the folder was hidden, and drops the folder rows.

Under the header, the **insert bar** offers the features of the focused view as small buttons (Σ, ∏, ∫, f′, tangent, piecewise braces, vector field, level curves, surfaces, table): a click types a template that draws on that view at the caret of the row being edited, or into the empty last row, and selects its placeholder so typing replaces it (f′ adds `f(x) = x^3` above when the sheet defines no f). Each view lists its entries as `inserts` in `src/views/<id>/`, and a test checks each one against the built kernel. While a graph tab is focused, the right sidebar's **Properties** has a **Reference** tab (a page icon): a search row on top, then every feature with its syntax, one sentence and examples, grouped, with the focused view's groups first, all collapsed until opened; typing filters all groups by syntax, name and description. The button beside the search collapses all (shown) groups, or expands them when all are closed. Properties' **Info** lists the graph: view, expressions (hidden ones noted), sliders, tables and points, axis labels, visible range (3D: cube range and camera angles), pinned markers, sliders playing, where it is saved and its id, updated at most four times a second. An example the focused view accepts inserts itself into the empty row. The long hint under the rows is replaced by one line whose **Open the reference** link shows that tab.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Cartesian | decimal, the x-axis in multiples of π or degrees, scientific; linear, semi-log or log–log | `y = x^2`, `x = y^2`, `f(x) = sin x`, `f'(x)`, `int(f, 0, π)`, `tangent(f, a)`, `sum(n, 0, N, x^n / n!)`, `{x < 0: -x, x^2}`, `tan x`, `x^2 + y^2 = 4`, `(1, 2)`, `[(1, 2), (3, 4)]`, `table`, `F(x, y) = (-y, x)`, `f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2` |
| Polar | circles and rays labelled in radians (fractions of π or decimals) | `r = 1 + cos(θ)` (type `theta` or `t` for θ) |
| Parametric | decimal | `(cos(3t), sin(2t))` |
| 3D | cube with floor grid, drag to orbit | `z = x^2 + y^2` or just `x^2 + y^2`, `x = y z`, `x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4`, `((2 + cos v) cos u, (2 + cos v) sin u, sin v)`, `(cos t, sin t, t)`, `(1, 2, 3)`, `F(x, y, z) = (-y, x, z/4)`, `tangent(f, a, b)`, `int(f, a, b, c, d)`, `int(f, a, b, c, d, e, g)` |
| Cylindrical | the 3D cube, drag to orbit | `r = 2`, `z = r^2`, `r = 1 + cos(φ)`, `z = φ / 2`, `φ = π/4`, `(r, φ, z)` points |
| Spherical | the 3D cube, drag to orbit | `ρ = 2`, `ρ = 1 + cos(θ)`, `φ = π/4`, `θ = π/3`, `(ρ, θ, φ)` points |
| Complex | Gaussian plane, imaginary axis in multiples of i | `3 - 2i`, `w = 2e^(iπ/3)`, `r(cos φ + i sin φ)`, `\|z + 2\| < 3`, `Re(z) ≥ 1`, `arg(z) < π/4`, `\|z - i\| = \|z + 1\|` |
| Complex 3D | complex plane as the floor of the cube, drag to orbit | `w = (z^2 - 4) / 4`, `f(z) = 1/z`, `Re(z^2)`, `Im(e^z)`, `1 + 2i` |

On the 3D sheet a triple says what it is by the names it uses: a triple in u and v is a parametric surface (x(u, v), y(u, v), z(u, v)), one in t a space curve and one of numbers a point. A torus is `((2 + cos v) cos u, (2 + cos v) sin u, sin v)`, a Möbius strip `((1 + v/2 cos(u/2)) cos u, (1 + v/2 cos(u/2)) sin u, v/2 sin(u/2))` with −1 ≤ v ≤ 1, a helicoid `(u cos v, u sin v, v)` (the placeholder of the empty row) and a cone `(u cos v, u sin v, u)`. Under the row, two range rows set u (`from`/`to`) and v (`from2`/`to2`), both 0 to 2π when left empty; the surface is shaded like the others and clipped to the visible cube. u and v are not reserved: on a sheet that defines `u = …` or `v = …` they stay sliders and the triple a point, as before, and a triple that uses only one of them, or t as well, still offers sliders for them. The fresh sheet keeps its paraboloid and helix; a surface would crowd it, so the helicoid is the placeholder instead.

Any other equation in x, y and z is an implicit surface F(x, y, z) = 0, drawn where left − right vanishes: the sphere `x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4`, the double cone `x^2 + y^2 = z^2`, the ellipsoid `x^2/4 + y^2 + z^2/9 = 1`, the hyperboloids of one sheet `x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 1` and of two sheets `z^2 - x^2 - y^2 = 1`, the cylinder `x^2 + y^2 = 4`. An equation that solves for one axis, `z = f(x, y)`, `x = f(y, z)` or `y = f(x, z)`, stays an explicit surface on the faster grid. The kernel extracts the implicit surface from a grid of 40 cells per axis over the visible cube by surface nets (the dual form of marching cubes: one vertex per cell the surface crosses, one quad per grid edge with a sign change), shades each quad by its normal and sorts it with everything else; a pole, where F changes sign without vanishing, draws nothing. The mesh is kept for the cube it was made in, so orbiting only projects it again; zooming extracts it anew (about 7 to 10 ms for the quadrics above in a Node harness against the built module). Small surfaces in a large cube are therefore coarse: zoom in for detail.

A vector field is written `F(x, y) = (P, Q)` on the Cartesian sheet and `F(x, y, z) = (P, Q, R)` on the 3D sheet, with any one-letter name (`F`, `E`, `v_1`) and the plot variables as its parameters in that order; the components may use sliders. A bare pair that reads x or y, such as `(-y, x)`, is a field too, and so is a bare triple in x, y and z on the 3D sheet: they were errors before, while a pair or triple of numbers stays a point, one in t a curve and one in u and v a surface, so no row that drew before changes meaning. Other parameters, other sheets or the wrong number of components report the rule. In the plane, arrows sit on a square lattice whose step is the 1, 2 or 5 × 10ⁿ value nearest a twentieth of the view's width, so 13 to 32 arrows cross the view at any zoom and they keep their places while panning (at most 2500; a tall view coarsens the lattice). In 3D a 7 × 7 × 7 lattice fills the cube, 343 arrows: projecting them costs about 0.1 ms per frame against 0.8 ms for one 64 × 64 surface in a Node harness against the built module, so orbiting stays smooth. Each arrow is centred on its lattice point; its length is the cell's width times the magnitude relative to the 90th percentile of the magnitudes in view, capped at 0.9 of the cell in the plane and 0.8 in space, so no arrow reaches into a neighbour's cell. The arrowheads have the shape of the complex sheet's arrows, scaled down. In the row colour, an arrow's opacity follows its relative magnitude (from 30 % to full); **Shade by magnitude** under the row turns that off (saved as `magnitude: false` in `graphs.json`). The field's name is typeset upright and bold. The empty Cartesian row suggests `F(x, y) = (-y, x)`; the fresh sheets keep their examples.

**Animate flow** under a vector field (plane or 3D, saved per row as `flow: true`, absent while off) lets its arrows move: each arrow drifts from its lattice point along the field for one cycle of four seconds and starts again, at its own point of the cycle (fixed by its lattice indices, so the lattice never jumps back as a whole), growing from nothing over the first fifth of the cycle and shrinking to nothing over the last, so the jump back is not seen. An arrow of the reference magnitude crosses one and a half cells per cycle and stronger ones are no faster; on the way it takes the direction and length of the field where it is, by six midpoint steps per frame. The kernel reads the time from `plot_flow(time)` (in cycles) before `plot_sample` or `plot_space`; the canvas that shows a visible flowing field asks for at most 30 frames a second on its own window's `requestAnimationFrame`, so a hidden tab costs nothing, and a still field is sampled exactly as before. Sliders in the components animate the field itself as before (play the slider); both can run together. An exported image holds the frame on screen.

On the 3D sheet `int` with more bounds is a double or triple integral drawn as a solid. `int(f, a, b, c, d)` is ∫ₐᵇ ∫_c^d f(x, y) dy dx: the row draws the solid between the xy-plane and the surface z = f(x, y) over that region and shows the value under it, the signed volume. `int(f, a, b, c, d, e, g)` is ∫ₐᵇ ∫_c^d ∫_e^g f(x, y, z) dz dy dx: the row draws the region itself, with z between e and g, and shows the value, which is the region's volume when f is 1 and its mass for a density f. The outer variable comes first, as the bounds are read from the outer integral sign inwards: a and b are numbers, sliders or constant expressions (finite, since they span the solid), c and d may depend on x, and e and g on x and y, so `int(1, -2, 2, -sqrt(4 - x^2), sqrt(4 - x^2), -sqrt(4 - x^2 - y^2), sqrt(4 - x^2 - y^2))` is the ball of radius 2 with the value 33.5103, and `int(1, 0, 2, 0, 2 - x, 0, 2 - x - y)` a tetrahedron. f is a function name (`f(x, y) = …` defined on the sheet), or an expression in x, y (and z). The solid is sampled on a 24 × 24 grid over x and y: its top and bottom are surfaces, its four sides strips between them, all clipped to the cube, shaded like a surface and ordered back to front with everything else, and its edges are drawn as curves so the faces read apart; where a bound has no value (outside the square root's domain) there is no solid. The value is the nested Gauss–Kronrod integral; all integrals inside one outermost integral share a budget of 30 000 halvings and stop at 10⁻⁸ relative, so a region bounded by square roots (whose integrands have vertical tangents at the ends) costs a few milliseconds and keeps about five digits, and no row can freeze the graph. The cylindrical and spherical sheets integrate over their own coordinates in their order, with the volume element included: `int(f, r₁, r₂, φ₁, φ₂, z₁, z₂)` is ∭ f · r dz dφ dr and `int(f, ρ₁, ρ₂, θ₁, θ₂, φ₁, φ₂)` is ∭ f · ρ² sin φ dφ dθ dρ, so `int(1, 0, 2, 0, 2π, 0, 3)` on the cylindrical sheet is the cylinder with 12π, `int(1, 0, 2, 0, 2π, r, 2)` the cone between z = r and z = 2 with 8π/3, and `int(1, 0, 2, 0, 2π, 0, π)` on the spherical sheet the ball with 32π/3 (`0, π/4` for the last pair gives the ice-cream cone). The region is sampled in those coordinates and converted to x, y and z, so it is round where the Cartesian form has square roots, and exact to 10⁻⁸. On the cylindrical sheet five arguments give the solid between z = 0 and z = f(r, φ), `int(4 - r^2, 0, 2, 0, 2π)` = 8π; the spherical sheet has no such height and refuses five arguments, as it refuses a function named without its arguments (write the expression). Faces without area (the side at r = 0, the cells at a pole) are left out. Typeset rows show the element before the differentials. Four or six arguments are refused. Inside other rows and on the flat sheets a double or triple integral is a number (`2 int(x y, 0, 1, 0, 2)` is 2). Rows that are not being edited show one ∫ per variable with its limits and `dz dy dx`. The ∬ button of the insert bar types `int(6 - (x^2 + y^2) / 4, -3, 3, -3, 3)`.

A function of x and y defined on the Cartesian sheet, `f(x, y) = x^2 - y^2`, draws its level curves f(x, y) = c (a bare expression in x and y and an equation in x and y keep their meanings: an error and an implicit curve). Under the row, the level count sets the most levels drawn in view (10 unless changed, at most 50): they fall on round multiples of a 1, 2 or 5 × 10ⁿ step across the values in view, from the 2nd to the 98th percentile so a pole does not swallow the range, and stay put while panning. A step typed beside it (any positive constant, such as `0.5` or `π/4`) takes over and draws every multiple of it in view (at most 200, coarsened beyond). **Label values** writes each level once along its curve where the curve is at least 60 px long, at 30 % of its length (then 70 %, 15 %, 85 %, half way) so labels of curves symmetric about an axis stay off it, and never over another label; hovering a level curve reads `f(x, y) = c`. The curves are traced by the same marching squares as implicit equations, once per level (about 3 to 7 ms per frame for ten levels in a Node harness against the built module). On the 3D sheet `f(x, y) = …` is the surface z = f(x, y), and every surface along z (`z = …`, a bare expression in x and y, `f(x, y) = …`) has **Level curves on the floor**: the same levels, for the values the surface takes inside the cube, are drawn on the bottom face of the cube (z = −range) from the surface's 64 × 64 grid, so they sit under the parts of the surface they belong to. All four settings are saved per row in `graphs.json` as `levels`, `step`, `labels` and `floor`, only once set, so older files load unchanged. The fresh 3D sheet shows its paraboloid with floor curves; the Cartesian hint names `f(x, y) = x^2 − y^2`.

Derivatives, integrals and tangents work with any function of one variable defined on the sheet, `f(x) = …` (its parameter may have any name). `f'(x)` and `f''(x)` (also `f′`, `f″`, `f‴`, at most three primes) are rows of their own or parts of other rows: `y = f'(x) + 1`, `g(x) = f'(x)` (and then `g'(x)`), `f''(2)` as a value. They are exact rather than difference quotients: the kernel evaluates f on truncated Taylor series (automatic differentiation in Taylor mode, `src/wasm/src/calculus.rs`), so the curves carry only rounding error at any x and any zoom (the tests compare sin′ with cos and (x³)′ with 3x² at several points and at 0.05 to 10⁶ px per unit). `|x|` has no derivative at 0, `floor`, `round`, `sign` and `mod` have derivative 0 between their jumps, and `x!` has none. A prime after a name that is not a function of one variable defined on the sheet (`k'(x)`, `sin'(x)`, `p'(x)` for `p(x, y)`) says so; four primes or more are refused (for a higher derivative define `g(x) = f'''(x)` and take `g'(x)`; nested derivatives compose up to the eighth order). `int(f, a, b)`, also typed `∫(f, a, b)` or `integral(f, a, b)`, shades the region between f and the x-axis from a to b and shows its signed value under the row; f is a function name, a derivative such as `f'`, or an expression in x (`int(sin(x), 0, π)` is 2, the placeholder of the empty row), and a and b are numbers, expressions, slider names or `±inf`. The value comes from adaptive Gauss–Kronrod quadrature (7–15 points, the piece with the largest error halved until the errors add up to 10⁻¹⁰ relative or 10⁻¹² absolute, at most 2000 pieces), with infinite bounds mapped onto a finite interval; a > b gives the negative and a = b gives 0, and an integrand that is not finite where the rule samples it gives no value. Inside other rows it is a number (`2 int(f, 0, 3)`) or, with x as a bound, the function `F(x) = int(f, 0, x)`, whose derivative is f again. Rows that are not being edited show it as ∫ with its limits and dx. `tangent(f, a)` draws the tangent line at x = a across the view with its point of contact and writes its equation `y = m x + q` under the row; f may again be an expression in x, and an undefined a offers a slider like every undefined name, so dragging it slides the tangent along the curve. Where f is not defined or not differentiable at a (`tangent(|x|, 0)`) the row says so, and `int` or `tangent` with an f that is neither a function nor a parameter asks to define it.


Sums and products are written `sum(n, a, b, term)` and `prod(n, a, b, term)` (also `Σ(…)`, `∏(…)`, and `Sum`, `product` in any case): the index first, then its first and last value, then the term, in the order Σ is read aloud (from its subscript n = a up to b) and the order of `int` with its variable moved to the front. They work anywhere an expression does: `y = sum(n, 0, N, (-1)^n x^(2n+1) / (2n+1)!)` (the Taylor polynomial of sin x), `Σ(n, 0, N, x^n / n!)` (of e^x), the square wave `4/π · sum(k, 0, N, sin((2k+1)x) / (2k+1))`, `prod(k, 1, 5, k)` = 120, `f(x) = sum(…)` with `f'(x)` (the derivative of every term, exactly) and `int(f, 0, 1)`, and `z = sum(k, 1, 3, x^k y)` in 3D; the complex sheet takes them too, with real bounds. The index is a plain name of its own (n, k, i, …) that is no variable of the sheet (x, y, … and z, i on the complex sheets) and no constant; inside the term it shadows a slider of the same name and offers no slider chip, while undefined names in the bounds, such as N, offer one like every undefined name. The bounds are numbers, expressions or slider names and are rounded to the nearest integer, halves away from zero (so a slider N stepping by 0.1 sums 3 terms from n = 0 at N = 2.4 and 4 at N = 2.5); a last index below the first is an empty range, 0 for a sum and 1 for a product. One sum or product takes at most 1000 terms: with constant bounds (numbers and sliders) a larger range, or an infinite bound, makes the row say "A sum or product takes at most 1000 terms; this one has …", so a slider pushed to 10⁶ cannot freeze the graph; bounds that change along the curve (`sum(k, 0, x, 1)`) give no value where they exceed the cap. Nested sums are capped one by one. The cap follows the cost of drawing: every sample evaluates every term, so the square wave takes about 4 ms per frame at N = 10, 22 ms at N = 100 and 0.2 s at the cap on the Cartesian sheet (900 × 640 px), and a surface with 1000 terms about 0.7 s per orbit frame. Rows that are not being edited show Σ or ∏ with `n = a` below and `b` above.

A piecewise function is written in braces with a condition and a colon before each value: `{x < 0: -x, x ≥ 0: x^2}`, or with a last branch without a condition that holds for every other x, `{x < 0: -x, x^2}`. It is a row of its own (`y = {…}` or just `{…}`), a definition `f(x) = {…}` (with `f'(x)` and `int(f, a, b)`), a surface in 3D (`z = {x^2 + y^2 < 4: 4 - x^2 - y^2, 0}`) or part of any expression (`2{x < 0: -1, 1}`). Conditions compare with `<`, `≤`, `>`, `≥`, `=` and `≠` (typed `<=`, `>=`, `!=` too), may be chained (`0 ≤ x < 1`) and joined with `and` and `or` (`and` binds tighter; `&&`, `∧` and `∨` also work); a comparison with an undefined side is false. The first branch whose condition holds gives the value; where none holds the function is undefined and the curve has a gap. A derivative takes the branch that applies at the point, so it is one-sided at a break. Braces without a colon inside keep their old meaning as parentheses (`{x + 1}^2`, `{1, 2}`), and an unclosed brace at the end closes itself; the complex sheets refuse piecewise functions. Jumps are drawn as clean breaks: the sampler bisects each step down to 1/4096 px and ends the path where the value still rises by more than 1.5 px (before, 8 px), so both pieces reach the break and no vertical connector is drawn (the same now holds for `floor`, `round` and `sign`). Open and closed end markers at a jump are not drawn: which piece owns the break follows from the conditions only, and at the sampled resolution the two ends sit on the same pixel column. Rows that are not being edited show a large brace with one line per branch, the value on the left and its condition (or "otherwise") on the right.
On the Cartesian sheet every visible curve y = f(x) (also `f(x) = …`) has markers at its zeros, its local extrema and its intersections with the other visible curves y = g(x). They are small neutral dots shown only for the curve under the pointer and for the selected curve, so a sheet of several graphs is not covered in dots: click a curve to select it (its markers stay), click empty space or press Esc to clear. Hovering a dot reads its kind and coordinates ("Maximum", "(1.1547, −3.0792)") in the readout, formatted like every other readout; clicking it pins that label, which follows the point as the view or a slider moves it (click again, or Esc, to unpin; at most 12 pins). Markers are found by the kernel (`src/wasm/src/markers.rs`, export `plot_markers`) for the visible stretch of x whenever they are needed and again after every pan, zoom, edit or slider change: each function is sampled on an even grid of at most 256 points across the view, and a bracket counts only when both ends lie on the same continuous piece of the drawn curve (the sampler's paths end at jumps, poles and gaps), so a sign change across a break of `tan x`, `1/x` or a piecewise jump is never a zero. Zeros and intersections are refined by bisection and kept only where |f| (or |f − g|) at the refined point stays under half a pixel; extrema by bisection on the exact derivative from `calculus::taylor` (golden-section search on f where f′ is undefined, as at the corner of |x|), and an extremum whose value is zero is a touching root, marked "Zero · Minimum" (two touching curves give an intersection the same way). Markers of the same pair of curves within a pixel are merged. The work per curve is bounded: at most 8 other curves, 256 grid points per curve, 64 refinement steps per bracket, 64 markers and 12 000 evaluations in all (an exact derivative counts 4), so a 1000-term sum costs about as much as one frame of drawing it, and only the hovered, selected and pinned curves are searched. x = g(y) rows, implicit curves, tangent lines and the polar and parametric sheets have no markers yet.

A table of points is a row of its own with an x and a y column. Type `table` in the empty row and press Enter, or click the quiet table button at the right of the empty row (both only on the Cartesian, Polar and Parametric sheets, whose points are x and y in the plane; the 3D and complex sheets do not offer it, and a table row there reports the rule). Each cell holds a number or a constant expression (`π/2`, `2a` with a slider a, `sqrt(2)`); a number written with a decimal comma or digit groups (`1,5`, `1.234,5`, `1'234.5`) is read as the number. The grid always ends in an empty row to type into, Enter or ↓ moves down a column, ↑ up, and Backspace in an empty row removes it; the column names above the cells are labels only. Paste from a spreadsheet into a cell, or into the empty row to make a new table: fields separated by tabs (what a spreadsheet copies), semicolons, commas (then decimals use points) or spaces, blank lines skipped, a first line of text over numbers taken as the column names, a single column read as y values at x = 1, 2, 3 …, columns after the second left out. A table holds at most 1000 rows; a longer paste keeps the first ones and says how many it kept (rendering only the rows near the scrolled position keeps a full table light). The points are drawn as the sheet's points and hovering one reads its coordinates; **Connect points** under the table joins them with a line in the order given, broken at a row without a point. Under the grid the row counts the points it draws, marks cells that are not numbers in red (an undefined name offers its slider) and keeps the table in `graphs.json` as `table: { head, cells }` with `connect`, both absent from older files, which load unchanged; the cells are sent to the kernel in a field of their own rather than as text through the lexer, so a pasted decimal comma or a tab never trips the parser. A list typed into a row, `[(1, 2), (3, 4), (a, 1)]`, draws its points the same way with the same switch. A table of r and θ on the polar sheet is not offered (its columns are Cartesian there too).

On the 3D sheet `tangent(f, a, b)` draws the tangent plane of the surface z = f(x, y) at (a, b): f is a function `f(x, y) = …` defined on the sheet (drawn as that surface) or an expression in x and y, and a and b are numbers, expressions or sliders (an undefined name offers one, so the plane slides over the surface). The kernel takes f(a, b) and both partial derivatives exactly, by the same Taylor-mode differentiation along x and along y, and the row shows the plane `z = p x + q y + c` and the normal `n = (−f_x, −f_y, 1)` under it. The plane is drawn as a square patch centred on the point of contact whose sides run along the plane, so it keeps its size however steep the plane is; its half edge is 0.35 of the cube's half edge (and the normal 0.4 of it), so both keep their size on screen while zooming. The patch is cut into 12 × 12 cells, clipped to the cube and shaded like any surface, so it is ordered back to front with the surface it touches; the point and the normal arrow are drawn on it. The normal belongs to the tangent row and has its own switch, **Normal vector** (on unless turned off, saved per row as `normal`), rather than a row `normal(f, a, b)` of its own: both are fixed by the same function and point, so one row keeps a and b and the details together, and the switch follows the existing row options (shading by magnitude, floor curves). Where f has no value or no derivative at (a, b) the row says so, and the empty 3D row suggests `tangent((x^2 + y^2) / 5 - 6, 3, 2)`, the tangent plane of the fresh paraboloid.

The cylindrical sheet reads (r, φ, z): r is the distance from the z-axis, φ the azimuth in the xy-plane measured from +x, z the height, so (r, φ, z) is the point (r cos φ, r sin φ, z). A row gives one coordinate as a function of the other two (`r = 2` is a cylinder, `z = r^2` a paraboloid, `z = φ / 2` a helicoid, `φ = π/4` a half-plane); a bare expression gives z, as on the 3D sheet. Under each surface two range rows set the other two coordinates: φ runs from 0 to 2π, while r (from 0) and z left open reach to the edge of the cube, as does `∞` typed as a bound. `phi` and `theta` both read as φ, `rho` as r.

The spherical sheet reads (ρ, θ, φ) in the mathematics convention: ρ is the distance from the origin, θ the azimuth in the xy-plane measured from +x (as φ on the cylindrical sheet) and φ the polar angle measured from +z, so (ρ, θ, φ) is the point (ρ sin φ cos θ, ρ sin φ sin θ, ρ cos φ) and `φ = π/4` is the cone about the positive z-axis. The physics convention swaps the two angle names; the hint text states which is used. A bare expression gives ρ, as on the polar sheet; θ runs from 0 to 2π, φ from 0 to π, and ρ left open reaches to the corners of the cube. `rho`, `theta` and `phi` are typed as words, and `r` reads as ρ. The fresh sheet draws an ice-cream cone: the cap `ρ = 2` with 0 ≤ φ ≤ π/4 on the cone `φ = π/4` with 0 ≤ ρ ≤ 2.

Both are their own views rather than row types of the 3D view because the sheets read the same names differently: on the 3D sheet φ and ρ are free names that become sliders, r is reserved for polar rows, and a bare expression is a function of x and y; a separate view keeps its coordinates reserved, its own surface rule and its own examples without changing what an existing 3D row means.

On the complex 3D sheet `w = f(z)` (or a bare expression in `z`, or `f(z) = …`) rises to |f(z)| and is coloured by arg f(z) around the colour wheel, red for positive real values; a real-valued row such as `Re(z^2) / 5` or `x y / 4` rises to its signed value instead, and a number sits on the floor as a point.

Rows not being edited are typeset as in a textbook: `x^2/3` shows a stacked fraction with a superscript, `sqrt(x)` a radical, `theta` θ, `<=` ≤, function names upright and variables italic; clicking a row shows the text to edit. Each row's gutter shows its number, which turns into a grip on hover: drag it to move the row up or down. The colour swatch beside it shows or hides the graph and its context menu picks the colour; the × on the right deletes the row.

Inequalities such as `y > x^2` or `x^2 + y^2 ≤ 4` shade their region, with a dashed boundary when it is excluded. On the complex sheet every number is drawn as an arrow from 0 and shown in both forms (`1 + 1.732i = 2·e^(iπ/3)`), `Re`, `Im`, `conj` and `arg` are available, and a relation in `z` (or its parts `x` and `y`) draws its region, or only its boundary with `=`. A row `a = 1` becomes a slider, and every undefined name offers one. The play button at the left of a slider animates its value between the slider's bounds; the chevron at its right opens the animation options under it: direction (→ forward from the minimum to the maximum, ← backward, ↔ back and forth), speed (¼× to 4×, where 1× crosses the range in four seconds) and **Loop** (forward and backward wrap to the other end, back and forth keeps turning; without it a run stops at the end, or for back and forth at the second end it reaches). The options are saved per slider row as `animation: { direction, speed, loop }`, only once changed, so older files load unchanged; whether a slider plays is not saved. The value advances by the time that passed, at most 30 values a second on `requestAnimationFrame` of the sidebar's own window, so a slow frame is skipped rather than queued, and lands on the slider's step. While playing, each value goes into the row without saving; `graphs.json` is written once when the animation stops, and the value stays where it stopped. It stops on Pause, at the end of a run without loop, when the sidebar turns to another graph, when another tab takes the focus, when the window or document is hidden, when the row is edited or the slider dragged by hand, and when the row goes away. Polar and parametric rows set their range below the input (default 0 to 2π); a surface over two coordinates has two range rows, saved as `from`/`to` and `from2`/`to2` (files without the second pair still load). User functions take one to three parameters: `f(x, y) = x y + 1` defines f, which other rows call as `f(2, 3)` or `f(x, 1)`. Rows may use `π`, `e`, `|x|`, `x!`, `sqrt`, `cbrt`, `ln`, `log`, `exp`, the trigonometric, inverse and hyperbolic functions, `min`, `max`, `mod`, `floor`, `ceil`, `round` and `sign`; `2x`, `sin 2x`, `sin^2 x` and `sin^-1 x` read as usual. Input is forgiving: Greek letters and constants may be typed as words in any case (`theta`, `Phi`, `PI`, `inf`), `30°` or `30deg` is in degrees, `log_2 x`, `root(x, 3)`, `∛x`, `x⁻¹` and `f(x) := …` work, parentheses and bars left open at the end close themselves, stray closing parentheses at the end are ignored, `0,5` outside parentheses is a decimal comma, and `x2` means `x²` for the plot variables `x`, `y`, `t` and `θ`. Enter adds a row, Backspace on an empty row removes it, the swatch shows or hides a graph and its context menu picks the colour.

The open-page button in the header opens the graph in the workspace: drag to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom about the pointer, hover a graph to read its coordinates (and, on the Cartesian sheet, its zeros, extrema and intersections), or use the arrow keys, `+`, `−` and `0` with the graph focused. Sheets are saved to `.valley/plugins/data/grapher/graphs.json`.

The three-dot menu of a graph tab exports what the tab shows: **Export as PNG** on every view and **Export as SVG** on the flat ones (Cartesian, Polar, Parametric, Complex). The file holds the current frame at the tab's size: grid, axes and their labels, curves, shaded regions, vector-field arrows, points and table points, level curves with their labels and pinned markers with their labels; the pointer's readout and the hover or selection markers are left out. The background is the theme's graph colour painted solid, so the file reads outside Valley. A PNG has 2 pixels per screen pixel whatever the display; an SVG is drawn by the same painters into real paths and text (CSS pixels, colours resolved to `#rrggbb` with an opacity, fonts ending in `system-ui, …, sans-serif`). 3D views export as PNG only: their surfaces are thousands of depth-sorted polygons, which an SVG would carry as hairline seams. Files are named after the view and the local time, `grapher-cartesian-20261113-004105.svg`, and go to the download folder set in General settings (Files and links) through the SDK's `files.downloadFile`; a PNG is handed over as bytes, which the host writes as they are (the vendored SDK 8.1.0 types declare text only, the host and the current SDK accept text or bytes). A short notice at the bottom of the tab says where the file went, or why it failed, and fades after six seconds. The sidebar has no export entry: it edits the focused graph, while the export is of the tab's canvas at its size and camera, and its menu is where the host keeps the tab's own actions.

## Code blocks

A ` ```grapher ` code block draws a live graph in a note. Each line is one expression, written as in the sidebar; a few setting lines, each `key: value`, may come anywhere:

````markdown
```grapher
view: polar
height: 360
r = 2 sin(3θ)
r = a + cos(θ) ; 0 .. 2π ; blue
a = 1 ; slider 0 .. 3 ; step 0.25
```
````

| Setting | Values | Default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `view` | `cartesian`, `polar`, `parametric`, `3d` (or `space`), `cylindrical`, `spherical`, `complex`, `complex-3d` | `cartesian` |
| `units` | `decimal`, `pi` (multiples of π), `degrees` (radians labelled in degrees) or `scientific` (m·10ⁿ) | the view's own |
| `scale` | `linear`, `log-x`, `log-y` (semi-log) or `log` (log–log); Cartesian and parametric views | `linear` |
| `height` | pixels, 160 to 800 | 320 |
| `center` | flat views: the point in the middle, `x, y` | `0, 0` |
| `span` | flat views: the units across the width | the view's own |
| `range` | 3D views: half the edge of the cube | the view's own |

Options follow an expression after `;`: a range `0 .. 4π` (optionally named, `t = 0 .. 4π`; a second range for a surface over two coordinates), a palette colour (`blue`, `orange`, `teal` …) or `#rrggbb`, `slider min .. max` and `step n` for a slider row, and the flags `hidden`, `floor` (level curves of a surface on the floor), `flow` (a vector field drifting along its arrows) and `connect` (points joined in order). Anything the block does not understand is named under the graph, with its line; so are the expressions' own errors.

The graph pans by dragging (3D views orbit), zooms with a pinch, the zoom buttons or `+`, `−` and `0` while focused; the plain wheel scrolls the note past it. Every slider row shows a slider with its play button under the graph. Moving it changes the block only: nothing in a block is saved, and the camera starts where the block says each time the note is shown. **Open in Grapher** copies the block's expressions into a new graph tab, where they are edited and saved like any other graph. The block picker lists ten examples (functions, sliders, axes in π, derivative and integral, equations and inequalities, polar, parametric, a vector field, a 3D surface and the complex plane).

## Settings

Settings → Grapher → Colours sets the order new expressions take their colours in: a list of palette colours (drag or the grip with the arrow keys to reorder, × to take one out while more than one is left, the add row for another palette colour, Reset to defaults for red, blue, green, purple, orange, magenta, cyan). A new row takes the first colour of the list no row of its sheet uses yet; the expression menu lists the same colours in the same order. Saved as the plugin setting `colors` (palette ids).

## Install

Copy this repository into `<realm>/.valley/plugins/packages/local/grapher`. The compiled `runtime/` (`index.js`, locales and `assets/plot.wasm`) is included; Valley does not install dependencies or compile plugins on load.

## Development

Use Node 24.19.0 and npm 11.17.0, plus rustup: `rust-toolchain.toml` selects Rust 1.99.1 with the `wasm32-unknown-unknown` target, and the build needs binaryen's `wasm-opt` version 133 (`VALLEY_WASM_OPT` or on `PATH`). Run `npm ci` and `npm run check` in this directory; it lints, typechecks, builds the WebAssembly module twice to prove the bytes are reproducible, bundles `runtime/index.js` and runs the tests against the built module. When the Rust source changes, the build reports the new module digest: record it as `runtime.wasm[0].sha256` in `config.json`.

The plotting kernel lives in `src/wasm` (Rust): the lexer and parser, the sheet analysis that resolves parameters and user functions, exact derivatives (Taylor-mode automatic differentiation) and adaptive Gauss–Kronrod integrals, the adaptive sampler for functions, polar and parametric curves, implicit equations and inequalities (marching squares, joined into polylines, and filled regions), complex arithmetic with principal values for the complex sheet, and the 3D renderer that samples surfaces on a grid, extracts implicit surfaces by surface nets, clips surfaces and space curves to the visible cube (parametric surfaces and surfaces in cylindrical and spherical coordinates on all three axes), projects them for the orbiting camera, shades them and orders them back to front. Run its unit tests from `src/wasm` with host flags, since `.cargo/config.toml` carries the WebAssembly link arguments:

```bash
RUSTFLAGS="-Cdebuginfo=0" cargo test
```

The TypeScript side (`src/`) holds the store, the sidebar and its typesetting (`MathText.tsx`), the plane and 3D canvases, the axis ticks and the localization in five languages. Each view has its own folder under `src/views/` (`cartesian`, `polar`, `parametric`, `space`, `cylindrical`, `spherical`, `complex`, `complexSpace`) with its icon, example rows, kernel mode, grid and default axis labels or 3D axis names, registered in `src/views/index.ts`. A new view adds its id to `src/views/types.ts`, a folder, a registry entry, a kernel mode in `src/wasm/src/sheet.rs` and the locale keys `mode.<id>`, `mode.<id>.hint` and `hint.<id>`.
